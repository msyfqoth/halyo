How to practise crypto charts without placing a trade

Recognising a chart pattern and understanding its limits are different skills. A useful practice session gives you something to change, a result to inspect, and a reason to question it.

Here is a short exercise you can try in Halyo's free browser demo. You do not need to connect a wallet, open an exchange account or place a trade. The aim is to explain what changed—not to find a setting that promises a profit.

1. Check which data you are looking at

The demo tries to load historical Bittensor (TAO) prices. If that data is unavailable, it offers clearly labelled, simulated sample prices so you can still learn the controls.

Read the label first. Sample prices are an illustration. Their returns, win rates and equity curve do not describe real TAO performance. Historical prices describe the past; they do not establish what a future trade will do.

2. Change one moving average

A moving average smooths recent prices into a line. A shorter window responds to a smaller slice of price history; a longer window smooths a larger slice. The demo uses a simple rule based on the fast and slow averages crossing.

Start with the displayed settings. Change the Fast MA from 10 days to 11 days and leave the slow average alone. Watch the explanation, returns, worst dip and number of trades recompute.

You are not being given a recommended trading setting. Changing one control at a time simply makes it easier to describe how that control affects this particular test.

3. Compare the training and test windows

The demo splits its dataset into a training window and a separate test window. Compare both. A rule that looks attractive in one window may look weaker in the other.

The separate window is useful for questioning a result. It is not permanently “unseen” if you keep using its result to select your settings. Once you tune against that window, you are using it to make decisions too.

A backtest can be shaped to fit a particular dataset—often called overfitting. A strong-looking result on those prices may not carry over to other periods. TradingView's strategy documentation discusses this limitation.

4. Look beyond the headline return

  • Worst dip: how far did the test fall from an earlier peak?
  • Number of trades: is the result based on only a few opportunities?
  • Costs: a result depends on the fees and slippage assumptions used. The demo uses fixed illustrative assumptions; actual trading costs can differ.
  • Comparison: does the apparent improvement survive a change of settings or period? One good-looking example is limited evidence.

A win rate alone does not tell you the size of winning and losing trades. Read it alongside the other results, not as a prediction of how often you will win in the future.

Finish with three questions

  1. Was I using historical data or simulated sample prices?
  2. What changed when I moved the fast average from 10 to 11?
  3. Why does that result still not tell me what to buy tomorrow?

If you can answer these in your own words, you have a concrete observation to build on. If not, revisit the label and change just one setting again.
